# Plausible grounds for access

[Español](accesibilidad.md) · [Full model](methodology.en.md)

Each relationship asks: **what supports presuming that B’s author or editor could know a pertinent form of A before composing the receiving part?** A quotation can answer that question, but is not required. The channel can be inferred and must be distinguished from an exact material itinerary. Access also does not establish actual reuse.

The registry documents 48 admitted connections and 9 pending candidates per scenario. Twelve admissions do not rely on explicit quotation: three from common authorship, four from literary transmission, two from editorial continuity, two from compilation and one translation. The eleven inferences have dotted arrows; Sirach’s translation has the translator’s testimony. The other 36 admissions concern identifiable quoted content, with the specific channel remaining unknown.

## Cases and conditions

| Relationship | Plausible ground for access | Condition and limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Thessalonians → 1 Corinthians; 1 Thessalonians → Romans | The same Paul could know content he had previously composed during continuing letter-writing activity. | Pauline attribution and documented temporal order are adopted. Neither reuse nor possession of the autograph is asserted. |
| 1 Corinthians → 2 Corinthians | Shared Pauline responsibility and continuing correspondence, with the first letter already composed. | Applies to Pauline content; possible later assembly of parts of 2 Corinthians does not automatically inherit that attribution. |
| Mark → Matthew; Mark → Luke | An earlier Greek account could circulate and be consulted; specific sequences and rewritings supply relationship-specific grounds. | Depends on Markan priority and Mark’s proposed role as a source. No exemplar, personal contact or library is identified. Added endings of Mark are excluded from automatic scope. |
| Luke → Acts | Reference to a prior account by the same narrator for the same addressee supports access continuity within a two-volume project. | The first account is identified with Luke; continuity of authorship or editorial responsibility is adopted. Edition variants remain open. |
| 1 Samuel → 1 Chronicles; 2 Kings → 2 Chronicles | The later Chronicler could consult preserved Hebrew antecedents, identifiable through specific narrative sequences and rewritings. | Adopts the published source-work reconstruction. Access to all of Samuel–Kings is not assumed, and no Temple archive is invented. |
| John, main text → epilogue | An editor adding an epilogue could know the body receiving it; the prior ending and reference to written testimony support this hypothesis. | Depends on chapter 21 being a later intervention. Editor identity and life dates are unknown. |
| Daniel, stories → assembly; Daniel, visions → assembly | Pertinent materials, including possible drafts, could be available to the responsible compiler. | The receiving stage is the hypothetical compiler, not every earlier author. Every chapter’s present form is not proved to predate 167 BCE. Greek additions are excluded. |
| Hebrew Sirach → Greek translation | The translator declares working with their grandfather’s work; published correspondences complement that testimony. | The exact exemplar is not identified with a surviving manuscript. Arrival in Egypt and translation completion are distinct events. |

## Sources and limits

NABRE introductions to [1 Thessalonians](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/intro/?search=1%20Thessalonians&version=NABRE), [1 Corinthians](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/intro/?search=1%20Corinthians&version=NABRE), [2 Corinthians](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/intro/?search=2%20Corinthians&version=NABRE) and Romans support proposed attribution, occasions and dating. Attribution and chronological order are complementary grounds, not additional witnesses created by repeating an introduction.

[Felix Just’s synoptic analysis](https://mobile.catholic-resources.org/Bible/Synoptic_Problem.htm) and Gospel introductions support the cases conditional on Markan priority. [Luke 1:1–4](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/luke/1) and [Acts 1:1–2](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/acts/1), with the introductions, delimit the receiving project without establishing a geographical itinerary.

The [1 Chronicles introduction](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/intro/?search=1%20Chronicles&version=NABRE) identifies work with Samuel and Kings antecedents. This review delimits [1 Samuel 31](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1samuel/31) / [1 Chronicles 10](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1chronicles/10) and [2 Kings 22](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Kings%2022&version=NABRE) / [2 Chronicles 34:8–28](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Chronicles%2034&version=NABRE). Editorial cross-references guide textual review; alone they do not establish a transmitting institution.

The [John introduction](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/intro/?search=John&version=NABRE) and [John 21:24–25](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/john/21) support the proposed editorial distinction. The [Daniel introduction](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/intro/?search=Daniel&version=NABRE) provides material diversity and the proposed composition setting for assembly. These are limited working reconstructions without invented biographies.

This review consults texts in translation and published editorial syntheses. It claims neither original manuscript collation nor an exhaustive investigation. Specific grounds and contextual conditions can depend on the same reconstruction; that dependence is disclosed in each assessment. See [the review audit](../research/access-review-audit.json) and the application’s precise locators.

## Open assessments

The nine pending relationships include Matthew → Luke, Deuteronomy → Joshua/Judges, Jude → 2 Peter, John → 1 John, Ezekiel/Daniel → Revelation, Esther translation and Daniel stories → vision authorship. Pending means assessment remains incomplete, not that access is impossible or requires a quotation or an exact surviving exemplar.

Daniel’s new compiler connections do not resolve whether the original vision author knew the stories. Keeping recipients distinct avoids inferring access retrospectively just because materials were later assembled.
